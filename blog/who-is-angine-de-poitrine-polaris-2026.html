Who Is Angine de Poitrine? Meet the 2026 Polaris Prize Winners

October 1, 2026

The masked Saguenay duo just became the first act to win both the Polaris Album Prize and SOCAN Polaris Song Prize in the same year. If you’re wondering where to start with Angine de Poitrine, start with “Fabienk.”

Angine de Poitrine are not especially interested in making themselves easy to explain. They perform in oversized masks and black-and-white polka-dot costumes, use the names Khn de Poitrine and Klek de Poitrine and describe their music with terms like “mantra-rock” and “dada pythago-cubiste.”

Underneath all of that is something much easier to understand. They are two musicians from Quebec making strange, tightly wound rock music that is difficult to categorize and surprisingly easy to get pulled into.

On Sept. 22, Angine de Poitrine won the 2026 Polaris Album Prize for Vol. II and the SOCAN Polaris Song Prize for its opening track, “Fabienk.” The sweep made them the first act to win both prizes in the same year since the song award was introduced in 2025.

If those Polaris wins are your introduction to Angine de Poitrine, they also provide a pretty good excuse to figure out what exactly is going on here. Thankfully, the music is more accessible than the costumes might suggest.

Who Are Angine de Poitrine?

Angine de Poitrine are Khn de Poitrine and Klek de Poitrine, two longtime collaborators from Quebec’s Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean region. Khn plays a custom double-neck microtonal guitar-and-bass hybrid with a loop station, while Klek handles drums and percussion.

Even the band name tells you what kind of project this is. “Angine de poitrine” translates literally to “angina of the chest,” which the duo have jokingly described as a kind of “dissonance-induced cardiac malfunction.”

The masks have an equally good origin story. After getting booked twice at the same Saguenay venue within a single week, the pair came up with disguises so audiences would still have a reason to show up for the second performance.

What started as a practical joke gradually became the band’s entire visual identity. The oversized heads, matching polka dots and anonymous pseudonyms are now inseparable from Angine de Poitrine.

The music underneath them revolves around repetition, odd rhythmic patterns, microtonal guitar and grooves that keep the technical side of the project from turning into homework. Angine de Poitrine can sound like math rock, experimental rock, psychedelia and something considerably stranger within the same song.

The important part is that the songs still move. Complexity is there, but it rarely feels like complexity for its own sake.

Why Does Angine de Poitrine Sound Like That?

A big part of the Angine de Poitrine sound comes from microtonality. Most familiar Western music divides an octave into 12 notes, while microtonal music can use pitches that fall between those conventional notes.

That helps explain why some of Khn’s guitar lines initially sound slightly bent, unstable or unfamiliar. Combine those tones with loops and repeating guitar figures, then place Klek’s dense percussion underneath them, and the band starts developing its own musical logic.

The result can be technically complicated without feeling academic. Repetition turns strange patterns into hooks, while dissonance gradually starts sounding like groove.

That tension is the Angine de Poitrine sweet spot. Their music sounds unfamiliar until, suddenly, it doesn’t.

Start With “Fabienk”

If you only listen to one Angine de Poitrine song, make it “Fabienk.” The six-and-a-half-minute opener to Vol. II is also the track that won the 2026 SOCAN Polaris Song Prize.

It gives you almost everything you need to understand the band. The guitar circles rather than settles, the percussion keeps shifting underneath it and ideas repeat just long enough to become familiar before being pulled somewhere else.

There are fragments of voice, but Angine de Poitrine are largely uninterested in conventional lyric-driven songwriting. “Fabienk” works more like a machine gradually locking its pieces together.

It is also the cleanest gateway into the rest of Vol. II. If “Fabienk” works for you, there is a very good chance the album will too.

Then Hear “Sarniezz”

Once “Fabienk” makes sense, move to “Sarniezz.” Where the album opener introduces the band’s rhythmic logic, “Sarniezz” pushes harder into the physical side of the sound.

This is where Angine de Poitrine become easier to understand as a live band. The repeated figures are not just exercises in complexity, because they accumulate momentum until the music starts feeling almost hypnotic.

Their KEXP performance offered a particularly good demonstration of that side of the duo. Recorded at Trans Musicales in Rennes in December 2025 and released by KEXP in February 2026, the session included “Sarniezz,” “Mata Zyklek,” “Fabienk” and “Sherpa.”

The contrast between the costumes and the precision of the playing is part of the appeal. As the band told The Guardian, “We’re not Lady Gaga. We’re not Elton John. We’re two random dudes.”

That self-deprecation makes the whole thing work even better. Angine de Poitrine may look deliberately absurd, but once the band starts playing, the joke never gets in the way of the music.

What Is Vol. II?

Released on April 3, 2026, Vol. II is Angine de Poitrine’s second album. It contains six tracks: “Fabienk,” “Mata Zyklek,” “Sarniezz,” “Utzp,” “Yor Zarad” and “Angor.”

That compact track list is deceptive because most of the songs develop gradually through repetition rather than traditional verse-chorus structures. The album works better as a continuous introduction to the band’s musical language than as six disconnected singles.

Khn handles the microtonal guitar work and looping, while Klek drives the record through drums and percussion. Despite the giant masks, fictional mythology and deliberately absurd presentation, the musical foundation underneath everything is remarkably disciplined.

That contrast is part of what makes Angine de Poitrine work. The presentation tells you not to take them too seriously, while the playing makes that nearly impossible.

How Did Angine de Poitrine Break Through?

Angine de Poitrine’s visibility expanded considerably in 2026. International performances, festival appearances, online clips and their KEXP session introduced the project to listeners well beyond Quebec’s experimental music circles.

By September, the duo arrived at Toronto’s Massey Hall nominated for both Polaris prizes. They left having won the Album Prize and Song Prize, turning what had already been a breakout year into something considerably bigger.

The attention creates an unusual opportunity for a band this strange. Listeners who might never intentionally search for microtonal experimental rock now have a straightforward reason to press play.

That might be the funniest part of Angine de Poitrine winning Polaris. One of Canada’s highest-profile music prizes has effectively become an invitation to investigate two masked musicians from Saguenay playing deeply strange guitar music.

What Should You Listen to Next?

Start with Vol. II from beginning to end, because Angine de Poitrine make considerably more sense as a full listening experience than as a collection of isolated singles. At only six tracks, it also does not require much commitment before the band’s internal logic starts revealing itself.

From there, go backward to 2024’s Vol. 1 and then watch the KEXP performance. Hearing the records establishes how the music works, while seeing the duo perform makes the whole strange package click into place.

Angine de Poitrine look absurd on purpose, but the musicianship underneath the costumes is anything but disposable. The Polaris wins might be what brought a lot of listeners through the door, but Vol. II gives them a reason to stay.
