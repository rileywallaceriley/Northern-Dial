Team Offense: Drake's First Beef

November 18, 2026

Before Kendrick said "Not Like Us." Before Pusha T made Drake be a "father." Before Meek Mill got Back to Back'd. Drake's first real beef happened at home, in Toronto, in 2006, against a crew called Team Offense, years before he was the biggest rapper alive, back when he was a teen-TV actor trying to convince his own city's rap scene to take him seriously.

The two crews

Team Offense ran eight deep: Aristo, Cali Snipes, Sese (the same MC who records today as Sayzee), Littles The General, Choice, Ceaza Leon, El Niño and Menace. They had East Coast roots in a Toronto crew (Littles was born in Nova Scotia), and they moved like a unit, on records and on the message boards.

Across from them stood the Wise Guys: Bishop Brigante, Ken Masters, Jonny Roxx, Young Tony (also known as Hush), JD Era and a young Drake, who released his first mixtape, Room for Improvement, in February 2006 and was still carrying Degrassi everywhere he went.

The battleground was the HipHopCanada message board, back when a forum thread was where Toronto rap argued its cases in public. Producer Dub J calls it "the infamous 2006 beef between The Offense and Wise Guys."

Boi-1da, the producer who would become Drake's right hand, had also worked with the other side. He linked with Aristo through Toronto's Battle of the Beat Makers, where Aristo was hosting, and produced two records for him, "East Meets West" and "Right Now," which Boi-1da has said picked up real airplay. Toronto was small. Everybody's circles overlapped.

What was the problem?

It was never pinned to one clean incident, and whatever thread once explained it went down with the boards. But the shape of it is clear enough from the records. The friction ran through Aristo, who had connections to Drake's circle (Boi-1da had produced for him), and the charge was right there in Team Offense's diss title: Drake, the kid from Degrassi, was treating rap as a "publicity stunt." That's a reading of the record, not a documented account of how the beef began. Nobody wrote the origin story down, or if they did, it's gone. What is documented is that Team Offense versus the Wise Guys played out in the open, on the HipHopCanada boards, for anyone in the scene to watch. Strip it down and the charge is an old one: the new kid with the TV show had to prove he belonged.

How it played out

The feud is remembered as a 2006 clash, but the blog era didn't keep receipts: the original release dates for these records were never properly documented, and the posts that would have settled the order are gone. This is the order the surviving accounts support.

1. February 2006 — the setup

Drake releases his debut mixtape, Room for Improvement. He is 19, still best known to most of the country as Jimmy Brooks from Degrassi, and Toronto's underground is deciding what to make of him.

2. Team Offense fires

"Publicity Stunt" opens with a Degrassi skit and an "Offense baby!" call, and puts Littles The General alongside Quake Matthews and Greedy, with verses from all three aimed at Drake. There was no subliminal anything. The title was the accusation, and the record said it to his face.

3. Drake answers, solo

"Goodnight & Goodluck" is Drake on his own, aimed at Aristo. No crew, no cover, just Drake and the guy he was aiming at.

4. The group reply

Then the Wise Guys answered as a unit. "Good Riddance" is the full crew over a Boi-1da beat: Bishop, Ken Masters, Young Tony, Jonny Roxx and JD Era, with Drake saved for last, walking in around the four-minute mark to close the record. HipHopCanada's 2013 history calls it arguably Drake's first win against his early critics, and listening to Drake close that record out, you can hear the Degrassi tag start to slip.

5. It spills offline

The beef also reached Toronto radio, with Drake, Boi-1da and Hush calling in to discuss the conflict with Aristo and Littles. Years later, The FADER was still pointing back at that phone call as the opening chapter of Drake's beef history.

6. Aftermath

HipHopCanada describes the conflict as short-lived and eventually squashed. Drake's rise settled the argument: Comeback Season in 2007, So Far Gone in 2009. Team Offense's members kept working (that story is below).

On the records, this was a genuine fight, and Team Offense landed punches.

What the blog era left behind

The frustrating thing about Team Offense is how little of the catalogue you can still put your hands on. This all happened during the blog era, and that era is mostly gone now: the blogs are dead, the file-sharing platforms they linked to are dead, and a lot of the music went with them. There was never a group tape under the Team Offense name. The crew's work lives under its members' names, and some of it is simply lost. The download pages for that era survive in the Wayback Machine as single images, the artwork and nothing else: teamoffense.com's page hid SendSpace links for Aristo's Trials of a Hustler and a Billz tape, and perfectstrangersmixtape.com pointed to Littles and Syph's Perfect Strangers. Every one of those download links is dead.

What survives tells the story anyway. Aristo had his own tape, Trials of a Hustler. Sese and Ceaza Leon made The Write Brothers together in 2010, and Littles and Syph made Perfect Strangers. Cali Snipes had a Rich Kidd-produced single, "Money 2 Burn," with a proper video. Littles and Kayo, billed on the upload as Team Offense, tore through "I Wanna Rock" in a studio freestyle in 2010.

And Sese never stopped: his YFRWN (Your Favorite Rapper's Worst Nightmare) arrived in October 2010 with half the old scene on it, and as Sayzee he released YFRWN 11 in the fall of 2026, twenty years after the beef.

Littles The General is not here to see any of it. Born Ryan Lorde, he was killed in Georgetown, Ontario, in October 2019, by then working the management and promotion side of the business he came up in. HipHopCanada's obituary for him recalls, fittingly, his place in Drake's first official rap beef.

Why it still matters

In 2006, a crew of hungry Toronto and East Coast MCs looked at the kid from Degrassi and decided he had to earn it, in public, on the boards and on wax. He did earn it, in part by surviving them.

The records run from a message-board war to Sayzee in 2026. Press play. This is part of what the beginning sounded like.
