Russell Peters: The Street Jam Years

November 22, 2026

Before the arena tours and the Netflix specials, Russell Peters was a working DJ at the centre of mid-1990s Canadian hip-hop. For three or four years he held down the mix on The Mastermind Street Jam, the only hip-hop show on commercial radio in the Toronto market, playing records and talking with the host.

This is the last thirty-one minutes of a May 1997 episode, beginning mid-song: a phone interview with a young Kardinal Offishall, a bill of Toronto acts, a ticket giveaway, a preview of Wu-Tang Clan's "For Heaven's Sake." Twenty-six minutes in, Mastermind says it out loud: "Russell Peters has been on the mix for the entire show." Peters is all over the tape: mixing, and talking on the mic between records.

Press play.

The Mastermind Street Jam aired on Energy 108, CING 107.9 FM, a commercial station serving Hamilton, Burlington and Toronto. It started in a Saturday slot, 5:00 to 7:00 PM, and later moved to Monday and Tuesday nights, midnight to 1:00 AM, where it was a live-mixed hip-hop hour and the only hip-hop show on the station. The Hip-Hop Radio Archive calls it "required listening" and points out the thing that made it unusual: it was on commercial radio. Mastermind, who had been doing radio since 1987, told the Toronto Mike'd podcast that hip-hop and R&B got almost no mainstream support in Canada. "It was all college radio. I had one show, you know, one hour, two hour program on Energy radio."

Peters was born in Toronto in 1970 and raised in Brampton from age four. He started doing stand-up in Toronto in 1989, but the DJing was not a sideline to it: daytime parties in rented clubs, thrown during the day because parents would not let their daughters out at night, with kids skipping school to be there.

They met around 1988 or 1989 at Bramalea City Centre, where Mastermind's cousin, a high school friend of Peters, ran a hip-hop clothing kiosk. Peters was already a DJ and already a fan of Mastermind's radio work. "He would come by once or twice and I'd put him on," Mastermind says. "He just said, I'm going to keep coming. And we did the show together for easily like three or four years, if not longer." Peters even turned up on Mastermind's album project, playing the game show host on the "Let's Make a Record Deal" interludes on Mastermind Presents Volume 50: Street Legal.

It was not the only time the show was his to run. A year earlier, on April 29, 1996, the whole program was his: "Welcome to The Russell Peters Street Jam." A full program of Big Noyd, Busta Rhymes, GZA, Heltah Skeltah, Mobb Deep, Redman and more, mixed live by Peters, with Mastermind's name still on the marquee. That recording survives too, in edited form: the talking was cut to fit a cassette side, so what is left is nearly all music.

Listen: The Russell Peters Street Jam, April 29, 1996

Then the comedy took over, completely. But the hip-hop never actually stopped, and neither did Mastermind, who still tells this story at length himself. When Peters hosted the Juno Awards in Calgary in 2008, in the middle of the telecast, he sat down at the turntables and scratched, live, on national television. Peters hosted the Junos again in 2009 and won a Gemini Award for the 2008 show.

That same year, Peters was already an arena headliner. He was also at DJ Qbert's place, scratching.

In July 2025, at Bastid's BBQ in Toronto, Peters played a full set of eighties and nineties hip-hop on a festival stage: Wu-Tang, Mobb Deep, A Tribe Called Quest, Pharoahe Monch. No stand-up. No talking. Then he stayed behind the decks and backed Smif-N-Wessun through "Sound Bwoy Burreil," restarting the track for the run-back.

His official bio still leads with it. Peters is billed, today, as "an ardent DJ and hip-hop junkie who tries to get on the turntables for at least a few minutes every day."

In September 2026, Wu-Tang Clan brought their final tour, Wu-Tang Forever: The Final Chamber, to the RBC Amphitheatre in Toronto, and in the middle of the show, RZA brought Peters out, after his own verse on "Tearz," to help lead the night's tribute to the fallen, the whole crowd singing the "Just a Friend" chorus over the beat. When "C.R.E.A.M." started, Raekwon and Method Man set him up to shout the first "dolla dolla bill y'all" before Raekwon's verse. A farewell tour does not hand that moment to a celebrity fan. It hands it to one of its own.
