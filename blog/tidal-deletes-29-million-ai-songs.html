Tidal Is Deleting 29 Million AI-Made Songs

November 25, 2026

Tidal is deleting 29 million songs. Starting December 9, the streaming service will begin removing fully AI-generated tracks, and it expects the purge to be finished by the start of the new year. After that, it will reject 80,000 to 85,000 uploads a day.

Tidal announced the move on November 24. Its music lead, Robert Anderson, put the reason in plain numbers: 43 percent of tracks delivered to Tidal over the past 90 days were detected as wholly AI-generated. On November 6 alone, the figure hit 60 percent of that day's deliveries.

The scale of what is already on the service is just as stark. About 27 million tracks, 6.3 percent of everything on the platform, are tagged as fully AI-generated. 96 percent of them have never been played once. A single generative platform, Suno, accounts for 94 percent of them.

This is not Tidal's first step. In June, the service started tagging AI-generated tracks and cutting them out of royalty payments. Anderson said that was not enough of a deterrent. The new policy goes further: the tracks come down entirely, and distributors are banned from delivering AI-generated music. A submission gets rejected if its metadata says the song was made solely with AI, and Tidal backs that up with audio detection from Ircam Amplify, the commercial arm of the French research institute IRCAM.

Tidal is the first major streamer to ban fully AI-generated music outright. Bandcamp did it first among the smaller platforms. Spotify and Apple Music have stuck to labelling and, in Spotify's case, a 1,000-stream threshold before royalties are paid.

For artists, the line Tidal drew is specific. Music made with AI assistance is still allowed. What is banned is music where AI is the sole output. An artist who uses AI tools in the process, for mastering, for stems, for a starting point, is unaffected. An artist whose entire track is generated has no home on Tidal after December 9.

There is a catch artists should know about. Detection is not perfect, and Tidal's own policy acknowledges false positives and false negatives. A human-made track that gets wrongly flagged can lose its place on the platform. Tidal runs a support process for disputing a flag, and artists with a release landing around December 9 should check with their distributor that the delivery metadata accurately says how the track was made.

The impersonation angle matters too. Tidal says it has already removed more than 65,000 tracks for suspected use of real artists' names, fake releases trading on a human artist's identity. The ban extends that protection.

For listeners, almost nothing they actually hear will disappear. 96 percent have zero plays. What changes is the clutter around the music people do listen to. Tidal's own explanation was that at this scale, real artists get buried under the flood. The deletion is, in Anderson's framing, housekeeping: a smaller catalog where human-made music is easier to find.

Whether the other major services follow is now the open question. Tidal just made labelling look like the cautious option.
