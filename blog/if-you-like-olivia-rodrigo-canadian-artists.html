If You Like Olivia Rodrigo, Try These Canadian Artists

November 11, 2026

Olivia Rodrigo's songs work because they read like pages that were never meant to leave the notebook. The details are specific enough to feel borrowed from real life, the emotions are bigger than the arrangements holding them, and she can move from a whispered piano ballad to a pop-punk tantrum without either one feeling like a costume.

SOUR did that first, written while she was still a teenager. GUTS did it again with better aim, funnier, angrier and more self-aware. The combination she is chasing, diaristic writing, real hooks, guitars when the feeling calls for them, is one Canadian pop happens to be very good at right now.

So this is not a list of Olivia Rodrigo soundalikes. It is six Canadian artists who each take one piece of what she does and push it somewhere she does not go. We built a playlist to go with it, three songs per artist, sequenced to be played front to back.

Hear the playlist:

For the feelings, at arena scale

Tate McRae

Tate McRae is the closest thing Canada has to a mirror image. She grew up in Calgary as a dancer first, reaching the finale of So You Think You Can Dance at thirteen, and started posting her own songs online not long after. "you broke me first" turned those bedroom songwriting clips into a career in 2020.

The difference is what she does with the feelings once they are written down. Where Rodrigo's songs often sound like they are happening in real time, McRae's are choreographed, literally. The heartbreak gets staged, lit and performed at full athletic capacity. So Close to What and songs like "Sports car" are what happens when the diary entry grows up and buys a tour bus.

If you love Rodrigo but sometimes wish the songs moved more, this is your door.

Start with: "Sports car" · Then hear: "Housewife" and "Just Keep Watching"

For the diary entry as protest song

Alessia Cara

Alessia Cara was writing Olivia Rodrigo's kind of song before Rodrigo released one. The Brampton singer built a following on YouTube covers as a teenager, then introduced herself in 2015 with "Here," a song about standing at a party she never wanted to attend, counting the minutes until she could leave.

It is the same instinct Rodrigo runs on: take a small, specific, slightly uncomfortable social moment and write it honestly enough that millions of people recognize themselves in it. Cara followed it with "Scars to Your Beautiful" and "Stay," a top-ten collaboration with Zedd, and a Grammy for Best New Artist in 2018, becoming the first Canadian to win that category.

Her writing is warmer and less interested in revenge than Rodrigo's, but the diary is the same size.

Start with: "Here" · Then hear: "Scars to Your Beautiful" and "Stay"

For the quiet songs that still sting

Lennon Stella

Lennon Stella grew up on television. Born in Oshawa, she spent her teens playing Maddie Conrad on Nashville alongside her sister Maisy, after the two of them had already become viral cover artists as Lennon & Maisy. By the time she released her debut album, Three. Two. One., in 2020, she had been singing other people's heartbreak on screen for most of a decade.

Her own songs are quieter than Rodrigo's, harmony-rich and patient, built for headphones rather than arenas. But she has pop reach when she wants it: "Takeaway" with The Chainsmokers and Illenium and "Polaroid" with Jonas Blue and Liam Payne both travelled far beyond the country-pop world she came from.

If the Rodrigo songs you replay are the soft ones, "drivers license" rather than "good 4 u," start here.

Start with: "Takeaway" · Then hear: "Polaroid" and "Smartest Person in the Room"

For piano-first storytelling

Ruth B.

Ruth B. built her first audience six seconds at a time. The Edmonton singer posted short song fragments to Vine, and one of them, a Peter Pan-inspired sketch sung at her piano, kept growing until it became "Lost Boy," her debut single in 2015.

Her writing still works that way, one image at a time, with storybook logic applied to adult feelings. "Dandelions" is proof of how durable that can be: released on her 2017 debut Safe Haven, it found a second, far bigger life years later and became her most-streamed song long after its first run.

Rodrigo's ballads are more confessional; Ruth B.'s are more like fables. The piano does the same job in both.

Start with: "Lost Boy" · Then hear: "Dandelions" and "28"

For the biggest voice in the room

Jamie Fine

Jamie Fine is the belter of this list. The Ottawa singer first broke through as half of Elijah Woods x Jamie Fine, winning the first season of The Launch in 2018 with "Ain't Easy," and her solo work keeps the same setting: a very large voice aimed directly at a feeling she is not interested in playing down.

"If Anything's Left," her solo breakout, is the clearest example. Where Rodrigo sometimes lets a song stay small on purpose, Fine goes the other way, building choruses meant to be sung back at her. Rodrigo fans who love the GUTS moments where restraint gets abandoned entirely will recognize the impulse immediately.

Start with: "If Anything's Left" · Then hear: "made for this" and "you're like"

For the next one coming

Sofia Camara

Sofia Camara is the newest door on this list, and the one still being built. The Toronto singer developed her voice in public, posting covers online and gathering a large following before her own songs started landing. She sings like someone who learned control by performing other people's biggest ballads first.

"Who Do I Call Now? (Hellbent)" is the song that best shows where she is headed: widescreen, voice-first pop about the phone call you make when the person you would normally call is the problem. It is easy to imagine a Rodrigo fan hearing it and feeling briefly at home, then realizing the voice belongs to somebody new.

Every list like this should end with an artist whose story is not finished yet. She is that artist here.

Start with: "Who Do I Call Now? (Hellbent)" · Then hear: "Hallucinating" and "The Last Encounter"

Where Should Olivia Rodrigo Fans Start?

For the closest transition, go from Rodrigo to Tate McRae or Alessia Cara. Both write from the same diary, one at arena scale and one from the corner of the party.

Lennon Stella and Ruth B. cover the quiet end, harmonies and piano respectively, while Jamie Fine is the pick when you want the volume turned up. Sofia Camara is the bet on where Canadian pop goes next.

Or skip the choosing and hit play: the playlist gathers all eighteen songs, three from each artist, sequenced for listening rather than reading. Then keep moving outward from whichever voice sticks.
