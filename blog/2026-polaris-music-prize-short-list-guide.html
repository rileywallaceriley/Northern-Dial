Polaris Music Prize · September 20, 2026

A Listener’s Guide to the 2026 Polaris Music Prize Short List

Ten albums, ten very different ways into Canadian music. Here’s where to start before the winner is announced Sept. 22.

Northern Dial artwork

The 2026 Polaris Music Prize Short List is unusually hard to reduce to one idea.

A microtonal rock duo from Saguenay sits beside a Halifax soul record built around Black Nova Scotian history. Winnipeg pop, Montréal avant-pop, Toronto singer-songwriter music, electronic R&B, Inuit experimental composition and Peaches being Peaches all share the same 10-album field.

That range is the useful part.

Polaris recognizes a Canadian album based on artistic merit rather than genre, sales or popularity. This year’s winner will receive $30,000 and will be announced Sept. 22 at Massey Hall in Toronto. For the first time, the winner will be selected by the full Polaris jury following presentations and debate, rather than by the smaller grand-jury model used previously.

You don’t need to hear these records as competitors, though. They make more sense as 10 different routes through Canadian music right now.

Angine de Poitrine · Vol. II

Saguenay, Québec · Microtonal rock

There’s probably no gentler way into Angine de Poitrine than accepting that normal rock rules are temporarily unavailable.

The Saguenay duo build Vol. II around microtonal guitars, percussion, repetition and rhythms that seem determined to rearrange themselves while you listen. The six-track album runs on its own internal logic, somewhere between math rock, mantra, psychedelia and an elaborate private joke that somehow became completely serious.

That tension is part of the appeal. Their visual world is strange, their instruments are strange and even their own descriptions lean into the absurdity. Underneath all of that, though, the music is extremely physical. Patterns lock in, mutate and come back around.

Start with: “Fabienk.”

Aquakultre · 1783

Halifax, Nova Scotia · Soul / R&B / Hip-hop

Lance Sampson uses 1783 to connect present-day Halifax to a much older Black Nova Scotian history.

The title points to 1783, when thousands of Black Loyalists arrived in Nova Scotia after the American Revolutionary War. Aquakultre turns that history into a 17-track record that moves through soul, R&B, hip-hop and storytelling without treating the past like a museum piece.

That distinction matters. 1783 is historical, but it’s also about lineage, family and what gets carried forward.

Start with: “Scotia Born.”

Begonia · Fantasy Life

Winnipeg, Manitoba · Pop

Alexa Dirks has a voice capable of making a Begonia record feel enormous, but Fantasy Life is just as interested in what happens when that power gets pulled inward.

The Winnipeg artist worked again with deadmen, the production duo of Matt Peters and Matthew Schellenberg, on an album filled with orchestral detail, strings, harp, brass and large vocal arrangements. The result can expand dramatically without losing the intimacy at the centre of Dirks’ writing.

“Deep Red Cherry Night” is a good example. It takes a simple image of love and domestic closeness and gives it the scale Begonia usually reserves for much bigger emotional eruptions.

Start with: “Deep Red Cherry Night.”

Bibi Club · Amaro

Montréal, Québec · Avant-pop / Electronic

Bibi Club’s Amaro begins with grief and keeps looking for somewhere to move.

The Montréal duo of Adèle Trottier-Rivard and Nicolas Basque made their third album after losing two people close to them. Instead of building something hushed around that experience, they push toward movement, community and the idea that dancing can coexist with mourning.

The sound follows that impulse. Avant-pop, electronic body music, dark wave and neofolk sit beside harpsichord, trumpet and ritualistic vocal arrangements.

It’s an album about death that keeps insisting on life.

Start with: “Amaro.”

Artist Profile

Charlotte Cornfield · Hurts Like Hell

Toronto, Ontario · Singer-songwriter / Folk-rock

Charlotte Cornfield has always been unusually good at putting ordinary details beside very large feelings.

On Hurts Like Hell, that perspective shifts after the birth of her daughter in 2023. Her sixth album circles growth, uncertainty, love, shame, awkwardness and renewal, with collaborators including Feist, Buck Meek, Christian Lee Hutson and Maia Friedman appearing across the record.

The arrangements stay relatively open, giving Cornfield’s writing room to do what it does best: make a small observation feel like evidence of an entire life happening around it.

Start with: “Hurts Like Hell.”

Artist Profile

Beverly Glenn-Copeland · Laughter In Summer

Canada · Folk / Ambient / Songwriting

Few records on this list carry more history inside them than Laughter In Summer.

Beverly Glenn-Copeland recorded the album with his wife and longtime collaborator Elizabeth Glenn-Copeland. It combines new material with reimagined songs from his catalogue, often in one-take piano and choir arrangements. The record was made after Glenn-Copeland disclosed his dementia diagnosis, and the couple have described the project through memory, devotion, grief and joy.

That context could easily overwhelm the music. It doesn’t.

The album feels immediate rather than archival, especially when Beverly and Elizabeth sing directly to one another.

Start with: “Harbour.”

Rochelle Jordan · Through The Wall

Toronto-raised · R&B / House / Electronic

Rochelle Jordan has spent years making the space between R&B and dance music feel like its own territory.

Through The Wall pushes further into that space. Across 17 tracks, the Toronto-raised singer moves between alternative R&B, house, pop and club music while keeping the record centred on resilience and self-belief. The album arrived in September 2025 as the follow-up to Play With the Changes.

The title is useful. This is music interested in forward motion, whether that means getting through something emotionally or finding the point where a song stops being reflective and becomes physical.

Start with: “TTW.”

Artist Profile

Les Louanges · Alouette!

Québec · Francophone rock / Pop / Soul

On Alouette!, Vincent Roberge brings guitars and joual further forward without abandoning the grooves that have defined Les Louanges.

The Québec artist’s third album mixes rock, funk, soul and pop across 15 tracks while working through questions of identity, mortality, love and life in Québec. It’s brighter and more rock-oriented than some of his earlier work, even as the writing takes on heavier personal and political ideas.

It’s also a useful reminder of how incomplete the idea of “Canadian music” becomes when Francophone records are treated like a separate conversation.

Start with: “Je confirme ma présence.”

Artist Profile

Peaches · No Lube So Rude

Toronto-born / Berlin-based · Electronic / Punk

After a decade between full-length albums, Peaches didn’t return quietly.

No Lube So Rude reconnects the Toronto-born, Berlin-based artist with the electronic, punk, industrial and dance vocabulary that made her disruptive in the first place, while shifting the conversation toward aging, bodily autonomy, sexuality and queer survival.

The jokes are still explicit. The politics are too.

What’s changed is the perspective behind them. Peaches is making this music from the position of an artist who’s spent more than 25 years watching conversations about bodies, sex and rights move forward, backward and sideways.

Start with: “No Lube So Rude.”

Tanya Tagaq · Saputjiji

Nunavut · Experimental / Vocal composition

Tanya Tagaq has already won Polaris once, taking the prize in 2014 for Animism. Saputjiji doesn’t sound like an artist revisiting old ground.

The 2026 record is confrontational from its opening track, “Fuck War,” then moves through compositions including “Razorblades” and “Imiq.” Tagaq remains difficult to compress into a genre because the work depends on voice, improvisation, composition, noise, breath and language as interconnected materials rather than separate techniques.

There’s political anger here, but there’s also something more intimate running through the record: pain, childhood, memory and survival repeatedly collide.

Start with: “Razorblades.”

What This Short List Says About Canadian Music Right Now

There isn’t one dominant sound on this year’s list, and that’s probably the clearest takeaway.

These records come from different cities, scenes, languages and traditions. Some are built around club music, some around guitars, some around voice and composition, and some around histories that don’t usually get placed side by side in mainstream Canadian music coverage.

That range is what makes the list useful. You can move from Aquakultre’s Black Nova Scotian history to Bibi Club’s Montréal avant-pop, from Rochelle Jordan’s club-focused R&B to Tanya Tagaq’s experimental work, without leaving the same shortlist.

The Sept. 22 ceremony will narrow the field to one winner. The listening doesn’t have to.
