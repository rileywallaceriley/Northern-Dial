Kardinal Offishall Returns With Firestarter Vol. 2, 25 Years Later

The Scarborough-born rapper's first solo album in more than a decade features T-Pain, Black Thought, Buju Banton, Idris Elba, Melanie Fiona and more.

Northern Dial artwork

Kardinal Offishall Artist Profile Stream the Album

Kardinal Offishall released Firestarter Vol. 2: Everyday, Sometimes on Sept. 25 through his Black Stone Colleagues label. The 10-track record is his sixth solo studio album and his first full solo release in more than a decade, with Kardinal producing eight of its songs.

The title goes back 25 years to Quest for Fire: Firestarter, Vol. 1, the 2001 album that included “BaKardi Slang.” Kardinal was born in Scarborough to Jamaican immigrant parents, and the mix of Toronto rap, reggae and dancehall that shaped his early catalogue remains central to the new record rather than being treated as a period detail.

Kardinal describes his original ambition as representing Toronto, Jamaica and his culture on the biggest platform he could reach. Firestarter Vol. 2 widens that network through a guest list that includes Black Thought, Stove God Cooks, T-Pain, Konshens, Glenn Lewis, Buju Banton, Idris Elba, NESTA, Melanie Fiona, Cipha Sounds and BJ The Chicago Kid.

Black Thought and Stove God Cooks appear on “40 Acres and a Ferrari,” while Cipha Sounds opens “All Nighter” before T-Pain handles its chorus and Konshens adds a dancehall verse. “Kiss Me When I” reconnects Kardinal with longtime Toronto collaborator Glenn Lewis over production from DRTWRK, and “Top 3” pairs him with Buju Banton and Idris Elba.

“My Brother's Keeper Part 2” shifts the album toward reggae and pairs Kardinal with New York artist NESTA. Kardinal produced the track and is credited as its sole composer-lyricist, while the song connects first-generation Jamaican-Canadian and Jamaican-American experiences across generations.

Melanie Fiona appears on “When You're in Love,” the album's relationship-focused ballad, while Idris Elba returns for “Bombay Sunsets.” The sequencing keeps the guest list moving between rap, R&B and dancehall instead of concentrating the collaborations into one section of the album.

“Where There's Smoke” brings the record back toward the hip-hop and dancehall overlap that has run through Kardinal's catalogue since the 1990s. Montréal producer Asethic built the track around hard drums and enough open space for Kardinal to switch between straight rap delivery and singjay phrasing.

The album closes with “Everyday, Sometimes,” featuring BJ The Chicago Kid on a Kardinal-produced arrangement. Its gospel-rooted chorus follows a record that has already moved through ambition, relationships, brotherhood and cultural identity across the previous nine tracks.
