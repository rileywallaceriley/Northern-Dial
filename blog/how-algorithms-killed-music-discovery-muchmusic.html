How the Algorithms Killed Music Discovery (and What I Took Away from the MuchMusic Era)

November 13, 2026 · By Riley Wallace

There was a time when discovering new music required a little patience, a bit of luck, and, in my case, a stack of VHS tapes.

Growing up, I’d rush home to catch RapCity on MuchMusic. I’d dig through my tapes, find one with enough room left, and get ready to record whatever caught my attention. Sometimes it was a video I’d been waiting all week to see. Other times, it was something I’d never heard before. The recordings weren’t particularly organized. Tapes were reused, songs were cut off, and videos sometimes started halfway through.

Some of the music that stuck with me the most happened before and immediately after RapCity.

VideoFACT helped finance Canadian music videos, giving artists an opportunity to reach audiences across the country. For me, MuchMusic meant discovering acts like Furnaceface, Bran Van 3000, and Sloan. These weren’t necessarily artists I was tuning in to hear, yet their music became part of my regular rotation. Watching MuchMusic gradually changed what I listened to, introducing me to artists and scenes I probably wouldn’t have explored otherwise.

Of course, MuchMusic had its limitations. Television programming came with gatekeepers, commercial priorities, and plenty of artists who never received the attention they deserved. The experience wasn’t nearly as democratic as nostalgia sometimes makes it seem.

Today, I can pull up an obscure independent release from 1987, discover an artist on the other side of the planet, or explore an entire discography in minutes. Streaming has made exploring music incredibly convenient, and I’ve discovered plenty of great artists through digital platforms.

But the recommendations are largely shaped by what I've already listened to. Streaming services track what we play, skip, and save, using that information to predict what we'll enjoy next. Listen to enough underground hip-hop, and the suggestions start leaning heavily in that direction. I might enjoy everything being recommended, but I'm still hearing variations on something the platform already knows I like.

My listening history is an incomplete picture of my taste. It only reflects the music I've encountered so far. When those preferences keep influencing what gets recommended next, it's easy to end up in a feedback loop. The platform suggests something familiar, I listen to it, and that choice gives it another reason to recommend something similar.

Even skipping a song can send a misleading signal. Maybe I didn't like it. Maybe I was distracted. Maybe it was something that would have grown on me after a few listens. The platform records the action without knowing the reason behind it.

I wonder how many artists never make it into my recommendations because my listening habits haven't given the algorithm a reason to suggest them. We've traded a shared channel for millions of individual ones, each shaped by our own preferences. There's an enormous amount of music available, but increasingly, a handful of platforms influence which parts of it we're likely to encounter.

Algorithms haven’t literally killed music discovery. I still find some of my favourite music through friends, DJs, independent radio, record stores, and music journalists. Someone recommends an album because they recognize an influence, remember a great live performance, or simply can't stop listening to it.

I've spent a considerable part of my life writing about music, interviewing artists, and exploring scenes that rarely receive mainstream attention. There are artists who made important contributions to local scenes but left behind very little documentation, records that disappeared almost immediately after release, and bands whose influence can still be heard in newer artists even when their names are largely forgotten.

That thinking helped shape Northern Dial. I wanted to build a place where people could discover Canadian artists and explore the scenes and musicians connected to them. Somewhere a visitor could arrive looking for a familiar artist and leave having discovered five more.

The site's Listening Paths explore connections between musicians across genres and generations, while Northern Dial TV brings music videos back into the experience. A big part of the project also involves researching overlooked Canadian artists and documenting their work, particularly when there's little information about them online.

I still have some of my old VHS tapes. Recording music videos was inconvenient, finding a particular song could be frustrating, and there was always the possibility that someone would record over something you wanted to keep. Some of the labels have faded, and I couldn't tell you what's on half of them without putting them into a VCR.

One has a handwritten label that reads “Rap City and various videos.” It's a pretty accurate description of how I collected music back then. I'd start out recording a show I loved and gradually fill the rest of the tape with whatever caught my attention.

I started Northern Dial because I wanted to give people more opportunities to discover Canadian music that way. There's a lot out there worth hearing, including plenty I haven't found myself.

I'd like to keep finding it.
